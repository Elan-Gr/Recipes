Meat Smoking Calculator

Pick a cut and a pit temperature, set your serving time, and it plans the cook backward — prep, smoke, rest, and a buffer — so the meat is ready when you are. Times are planning estimates from the master smoking chart, not promises; real cooks vary by 25 percent or more, and the probe is the only instrument that knows whether your piece of meat is done. Per-pound cuts scale with weight; per-rack cuts do not — a second rack on the same grate takes the same time as one. Modeled on the cook-time calculator and master chart at SmokerCalc.com (the same data file behind MeatSmokingCalculator.com).

    Notes

    • Hours per pound is a planning device, not physics. What really sets the clock is thickness — heat travels inward from the surface, so a thick, square 12 lb brisket will beat a long, flat 14 lb one to temperature every time.
    • The stall: a plateau, usually between 150°F and 165°F internal, where a large cut stops climbing for two to six hours. Moisture wicks to the surface and evaporates, and evaporation removes heat about as fast as the fire adds it. Wait it out for the firmest bark, wrap in butcher paper (breathes, keeps bark reasonably firm), or wrap in foil (kills the stall hardest, partly steams the bark soft). Most cooks wrap between 160°F and 170°F, or when the bark color is where they want it.
    • Work backward from serving time against the slow end: take the slow estimate, add the rest, add a buffer of at least two hours on anything longer than six hours. A cut that finishes early is a solved problem — wrap it, put it in a dry cooler packed with towels, and it holds above 140°F for hours. A cut that finishes late is not solvable.
    • Raising the pit 25°F removes about 15 percent of the cook (225°F to 250°F) and about 17 percent more (250°F to 275°F). For most cooks 250°F is the better default; reserve 225°F for when you want the longest smoke exposure and have the hours spare. Use 275°F or higher for poultry so the skin renders instead of turning rubbery.
    • Two briskets on one smoker do not take twice as long. Time scales with the thickness of an individual piece, not the total weight on the grate. Add about an hour of buffer for a full cooker.
    • Every look costs roughly 15 minutes of recovery on a charcoal or electric cooker. Use a leave-in probe and stop peeking. Probe the thickest part of the muscle, not touching bone and not in a fat seam.
    • Keep hot-held meat at or above 140°F from the pit to the plate. Below 140°F, the clock starts — health codes give a cumulative 2 to 4 hours in the danger zone before food must be served or discarded.
    • Beef burnt ends hours are the second cook only, after a point has already been smoked and cubed. Budget the full point cook ahead of it.
    • Pull temperature is a texture target; the USDA minimum is a safety number. On a brisket they are 58 degrees apart, and the gap represents hours of cooking that exist purely to render collagen. On a whole turkey they are identical, which is why poultry has no equivalent margin for error.
