Guide to Chocolate Tempering - King Arthur Baking

Source: King Arthur Baking — <https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/guide-to-chocolate-tempering>

Tempered chocolate has been melted, cooled, and handled so that very specific fat crystals (called beta crystals) form. The result: chocolate that's shiny, snaps when broken, and isn't tacky to the touch. Virtually all chocolate you buy is already in temper.

It is possible, with great care, to very gently melt tempered chocolate to precisely 90°F and keep it in temper — but it's difficult, and most people melt and re-temper instead.

The tempering process

Regardless of method, the sequence is the same:

  1. Melt the chocolate to a temperature that melts all the different types of fat crystals present.
  2. Cool the chocolate while stirring, often with the addition of more chocolate that's already in temper, so a uniform structure of crystals reforms.
  3. Once the chocolate is at the right temperature, use it to line molds, coat centers, or dip cookies in, and set out at cool room temperature. If properly in temper, it sets within 3 to 5 minutes.

The seeding method

Melt chocolate, then chop more chocolate and add it to "seed" the melted chocolate. The stable crystals in the chopped chocolate encourage stable beta crystals to form in the melted chocolate. Stirring is very important — it keeps the smallest beta crystals possible in suspension.

  • Cool to 88° to 90°F (27°C) while stirring continuously.
  • If the bowl still has chunks at that temperature, gently warm it to melt the remaining chunks (over warm water, or even with a hair dryer).
  • If the chocolate is too warm, add more chunks a few at a time while stirring to cool to the correct working temperature.
  • After cooling, hold the chocolate at working temperature for dipping, pouring, spreading, or piping.

You can do all of this with a simmering water bath, a bowl, a thermometer, a rubber spatula (to stir with), and a knife (to chop the chocolate).

Other tempering methods

Block seeding

Add a large chunk of tempered chocolate to warm, melted chocolate and stir until the melted chocolate is cooled to temper. Then remove the block (it can be reused). Simple but slightly more time consuming; the advantage is an easily discernible chunk you can remove from your working chocolate.

Chocolate on marble (tabling)

For relatively small amounts of chocolate; fast and efficient. Melt to 122°F/50°C for dark, 105°F/40°C for milk or white (removes all existing cocoa butter crystals). Pour 1/2 to 2/3 of the melted chocolate onto a scrupulously clean, absolutely dry marble slab; spread back and forth with a metal spatula until it begins to thicken. It takes on a paste-like consistency and dull color as the beta crystals form. Add this mass back to the remaining melted chocolate to seed and cool it, stirring constantly. Maintain at working temperature (86° to 90°F for dark; 84° to 86°F for milk or white), stirring frequently.

Incomplete melting

Melt about 80% of a volume of chopped tempered chocolate in the microwave or a warm water bath. This relies on the remaining unmelted chocolate as the seed. Make sure the melted portion doesn't get above 97°F/36°C. Use low power and short bursts in the microwave, stirring in between (microwaves create hot spots). Chop the chocolate small so it melts evenly.

Testing for temper

Dip a metal tool or spoon into the chocolate when it reaches 90°F. Keep stirring while the coating on the utensil sets: if tempered, it sets within 3 to 5 minutes at normal room temperature, with a satiny shine and no streaks. If not in full temper, it takes longer to harden, looks dull or streaky, and leaves a fingerprint if touched.

Holding the working temperature: place the bowl over warm (90 to 95°F) water (be absolutely sure no water gets into the chocolate or it will seize). As the crystals form, they release heat — place dipped chocolate in a cool place (65°F is optimal). Dipping in a hot kitchen or in high summer without air conditioning can be futile.

Chocolate terms

Bloom

When chocolate is exposed to moisture or heat, sugar or fat can fall out of suspension and recrystallize on the surface — "bloom." Bloomed chocolate looks dusty, grayish, streaked, or freckled; it's safe to eat but isn't very attractive.

% Cacao

The portion of the chocolate from the cacao tree. This number has little to do with the quality of the chocolate inside; the remainder is sugar, milk solids, butterfat, lecithin, etc.

Seizing

Chocolate can "seize" — turn into a grainy, lumpy mess. Melted chocolate is essentially a dry substance (no water in it). If even a few drops of water get in, they're immediately taken up by the sugar and cacao, creating rigid lumps. The only fix is to add a lot more liquid until the chocolate is saturated and becomes a syrup. Seized chocolate can't be tempered or used as pure chocolate.

Cocoa butter

The fat found in cacao beans; solid at room temperature.
