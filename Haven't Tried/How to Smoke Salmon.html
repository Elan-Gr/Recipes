How to Smoke Salmon

From Hank Shaw, Honest Food (Hunter Angler Gardener Cook) — a hot-smoking recipe, not cold smoking. Works with any salmonid: king, sockeye, coho, pink, chum, dolly varden, kokanee, steelhead, Lahontan trout, or farmed Atlantic salmon. The keys are a proper brine, a fully formed pellicle, and gentle heat so the albumin doesn’t bleed.

What You Need

  • A smoker you can roughly control the temperature of (he uses a Traeger and a Bradley)
  • Wood — alder is his preference; apple, cherry, oak, or maple also work
  • Kosher salt — Diamond Crystal; do not use table salt (iodide and anti-caking agents give an off flavor)
  • Something sweet for basting — birch syrup, real maple syrup, or honey
  • A large flat plastic container for brining, a wire rack, and a basting brush

Ingredients

  • 5 pounds salmon, trout, or char, cut into pieces about 1/4 to 1/2 pound each, skin on
  • Birch or maple syrup (or honey) for basting

Dry Brine

  • Equal parts light brown sugar and kosher salt by weight (1:1), enough to coat the fillets evenly on all sides

Instructions

  1. Mix equal parts light brown sugar and kosher salt by weight. Coat the fillets evenly on all sides with the dry brine, place in a non-reactive container (plastic or glass), cover, and refrigerate for 36 to 48 hours.
  2. Take the fish out, rinse the brine off under cold running water, and pat dry. Set the fillets on a wire rack, skin side down, somewhere cool and breezy — ideally right under a ceiling fan on high, or outside in a cool (60°F or cooler), breezy place. Let dry 2 to 4 hours, or up to overnight in the fridge. You want the surface to develop a shiny skin called a pellicle: a thin, lacquer-like layer that seals the fish and gives the smoke something to stick to. Don’t skip this step — it’s the one most beginning smokers fail to do. Once the pellicle has formed, you can refrigerate the fish for a few hours and smoke it later if you’d like.
  3. Slick the skin of the fish with some oil so it won’t stick to the smoker rack.
  4. Start with a small fire and bring the temperature up gradually — high heat is what causes the white albumin bleed. Start the smoker between 140°F and 150°F for up to an hour, then finish at 175°F for a final hour or two. Put water in the drip pan to keep the temperature down; if your smoker runs very hot, put ice in the tray. Your goal is an internal temperature of about 130°F to 140°F. Keep the smoke on the whole time; for a lighter smoke, finish without smoke or in a 200°F oven.
  5. After an hour in the smoker, baste the fish with birch or maple syrup (or honey), and repeat every hour. Basting also brushes away any albumin that forms — a little is normal, a lot means the heat was too high.
  6. Once smoked, let the fish rest on the cooling rack for an hour before refrigerating. Wrapped in plastic, smoked fish keeps 10 days in the fridge; vacuum-sealed, up to 3 weeks; frozen, up to a year.

Notes: Use skin-on fillets — without the skin, the salmon loses too much moisture over the long smoke. If the heat gets away from you and you end up with a white mess on the salmon, all is not lost — flake it out and make salmon salad; the mayonnaise masks any dryness. The process takes a while, so fill up the smoker — it doesn’t care if it’s full or half-empty.
